Wang's case is that Meta wasn't on the frontier when he arrived, and the only way to catch up in months rather than years was to **rebuild the entire research stack from scratch** under three principles — higher compute per researcher, ruthless talent density, and very big research bets — wrapped in a genuine organizational belief that *superintelligence is actually coming*. MuseSpark is the first data point on that rebuilt ladder; he calls it the **appetizer, not the entrée**.

- **The Tahoe deal happened because of a years-long relationship with Zuckerberg**, not a sudden bidding war; Mark had become "AGI-pilled" and knew Llama wasn't on the right trajectory, and the open-ended brainstorm landed at "do this together" because compute and frontier model rights are the centre of gravity of the next decade.
- **Llama's real problem was conviction**, not talent or compute. Other big labs are built on the premise that superintelligence is imminent; Meta's AI org wasn't. Step one was to take that premise seriously and rebuild every other assumption around it.
- **The recruiting frenzy looks mercenary from the outside and isn't, from the inside.** Most researchers Wang hired could already print money where they were; what they wanted was compute per researcher, a small cracked team, and room for ambitious bets. Multiple visitors from other labs tell him MSL "feels like early OpenAI or early Anthropic."
- **MuseSpark's headline surprise is token efficiency** — comparable scores with far fewer tokens — which Wang attributes to *the privilege of building a clean stack the right way*. It's not state-of-the-art across the board and was never meant to be; the next model on the scaling ladder is what he's actually excited about, and it's "coming months" away.
- **Open source is still in, with a safety filter.** MuseSpark tripped the bio/chem/cyber/loss-of-control preparedness checks and so wasn't open-sourced; Meta is actively developing open-sourceable variants.
- **The personal stuff he's willing to address**: Yann LeCun's "young and inexperienced" jab (they patched it up in India), Sam Altman's recent unflattering quotes (he hopes the industry's animosities subside as we get closer to superintelligence), and the soup rumour (won't confirm whether Zuck made it).
- **Strategic stakes beyond the model**: a humanoid robotics acquisition (Assured Robot Intelligence) because physical superintelligence is on the natural continuum once you take short timelines seriously; a health-superintelligence push with CZI; a research thread on BCI ("Tribe B2") showing zero-shot generalisation of brain prediction; and a "country-of-geniuses → **economy of agents in a data center**" framing where Meta's billions of consumers and hundreds of millions of small businesses are the unique substrate.
- **Where he actually lands philosophically**: safety is table stakes; the destination is *personal superintelligence* in everyone's hands; **model welfare** matters and almost nobody is talking about it enough.

## 1. Cold open: the man who went into hiding (0:00)

The framing is the joke of the episode. Alex Wang, "chief of Meta's artificial intelligence efforts," has been in "AI protection program" for ten months — barely a public word since Zuckerberg quasi-acquired Scale and put him in charge of Meta's AI push. Vance and Robison preview the territory they want to cover: where Meta now sits philosophically on AI (Wang at Scale was famously "Switzerland" of the industry — loud on some things, careful on others), what was actually done with the small mountain of recruiting money, and where the new model fits. The headline reasons to listen, even before he says anything substantive: he just released a new model (MuseSpark), and nobody in the press has heard him explain himself yet.

---

## 2. Inside MSL: TBD, PAR, FAIR, and Meta Compute (2:02)

Wang lays out the org chart, which the outside world has only seen in fragments. The umbrella is **Meta Superintelligence Labs (MSL)**, which he oversees. Inside it sit four pieces:

- **TBD** — the large-model research lab, where the headline researchers and infrastructure engineers live; "somewhat infamous" in the discourse but, structurally, the frontier-model effort. They all report up to Wang, and he is personally hands-on with TBD.
- **PAR (Product and Applied Research)** — run by **Nat Friedman**, responsible for the products built on the models and for actually shipping them to the world.
- **FAIR** — continues to do exploratory and scientific research. Wang singles out the cross-over work he's most excited about: AI models that understand the brain, and a *universal model for atoms* used in computational chemistry.
- **Meta Compute** — led by **Daniel Gross**, focused on the long-horizon GPU and data-centre planning the whole endeavour depends on.

A fifth name worth knowing: **Shengjia Zhao**, chief scientist of MSL, who oversees the scientific agenda across the whole umbrella. The personal map under the org map is older than the deal — Friedman was one of Wang's first angels at Scale years ago, Daniel Gross he met around the same time, and Shengjia he knew before MSL but has gotten close to since.

He has physically moved too — from his old San-Francisco-light identity down to South Bay; "for me the city now is Palo Alto." A small detail, but a tell about how full-in this is.

## 3. The Tahoe conversation and why Meta was the bet (6:23)

This is the section where the deal stops being a meme and starts being a strategy. The relationship with Zuckerberg is the precondition: Wang has known Mark for years, had been collecting advice from him during Scale, and they had been talking about AI long before the current craze — back when Scale's AI work was mostly self-driving (2016 vintage).

The pivotal conversation was about a year before the public news. By then Mark had become *"increasingly AGI-pilled"* and had concluded two things at once: AI was going to totally transform Meta, and AI was a once-in-a-lifetime technology that warranted a very big bet. He had also concluded, publicly, that **Llama was not on the trajectory the company needed**. The brainstorm Wang describes is open-ended at first — "how could we work together more closely?" — and lands in a shape that was *good for Scale, good for Meta, and let them go build the most important technology of our time together*. The artefact of the new direction is Zuckerberg's "personal superintelligence" memo, written around the same time; that became the north star.

When Vance presses on why this jumped from "advisor" to "run-the-whole-thing for an enormous price," Wang gives the strategic argument that, in his head, made the swap make sense. Two shifts in his thinking:

- **Whoever builds the models gets greater and greater rights** — both economic and product — to build everything around them. The model layer keeps absorbing more of the value because the research pace is so fast; being at a place that builds the models is increasingly the most exciting seat in the ecosystem.
- **This phase of technology boils down to compute.** That creates a stratification: it is now wrong to lump all tech companies together; companies *with* a lot of compute can build things that companies *without* simply cannot. Meta has compute at a scale that matches Zuckerberg's willingness to bet, and that combination — bold strategist plus an enormous compute pool — is what made the seat worth taking.

The throughline is not "Meta needed me"; it's "the right place to be in this decade is where the compute and the conviction meet, and that turned out to be Meta."

## 4. Rebuilding a frontier lab in nine months: three principles (12:50)

Wang's diagnosis when he arrived: Llama was not on the same trajectory as the frontier, and the gap could not be closed by tweaks. The whole effort needed a reset.

The diagnosis went one level deeper than the technical state. Leading labs are built on the *premise* that superintelligence is coming and is reachable, and they construct their org, their plan, and their business around that premise. Meta had smart people working on AI, but the org wasn't shaped by that conviction — and that, more than any specific bug in the stack, was the foundational problem. **Step one was therefore to take superintelligence seriously and then rebuild every other assumption around it.** The new name (Meta *Superintelligence* Labs) is itself the commitment.

From there, four principles for the rebuild:

1. **Take superintelligence seriously.**
2. **Technical voices are loudest.**
3. **Scientific rigour; focus on basics.**
4. **Make big bets.**

The actionable theory behind the shape of the lab boils down to **three levers for catching the frontier fast**:

- **Higher compute per researcher.** Big labs have lots of compute but spread it so many ways that any individual researcher is throttled. A more focused, smaller team with more compute per head moves faster.
- **Talent density.** A small cracked team always beats a large one with distributed responsibility. (His framing: the human organisation lesson everyone has to keep relearning.)
- **Very ambitious research bets.** Industry-agreed in principle but rarely funded in practice — bets that, if they pay off, change paradigms. MSL allocates a large chunk of compute to those, alongside the work on competitive frontier models.

On *what they care about* at the bet level: Wang reframes the goal as **personal superintelligence** — agents that empower the billions of consumers and the hundreds of millions of small businesses on Meta's platforms. The sub-capabilities needed for that drive the research stack: very strong agent capabilities, very strong coding, real multimodality, and the harder open questions — long-running agents, agent memory, and tasks of growing complexity executed on the user's behalf.

When Vance puts the obvious challenge — that from the outside it looks "mercenary," a bunch of high-priced hires bolted onto a giant company — Wang's response is that the *external narrative* and the *inside culture* are sharply different. The recruiting *looked* mercenary because it happened in a sprint ("we knew if we wanted great models we needed the team yesterday"). The day-to-day, by his account and by the verdict of other-lab visitors, is closer to early OpenAI or early Anthropic: cracked team, low bloat, a real say in research direction. His own line: most of these hires could already make enormous money where they were — what moved them was compute per head, talent density, and the freedom to take big swings.

## 5. Soup, Sam Altman, Yann LeCun, and the "too young" knock (24:35)

The personal segment, which Wang handles with a uniform de-escalation strategy: acknowledge, redirect to the work.

- **The soup rumour** (Zuck supposedly cooking soup as part of the recruiting blitz): he can't confirm whether the soup actually existed, but he affirms the underlying point — recruiting was deeply individualised, candidates needed to *see* that the leadership cared about their specific research direction, and Meta had to overcome a default that many researchers simply didn't know what to make of its AI effort.
- **Sam Altman.** Wang notes Vance had texted Sam ahead of the interview and got unflattering quotes back. He doesn't litigate it; instead he offers the *as we get closer to superintelligence the animosities should subside* line, and adds that he sees it as part of his responsibility to be thoughtful about how the technology is deployed.
- **Yann LeCun's "young and inexperienced, more people will leave" jab** (shortly after LeCun's own departure). Wang says he saw Yann in India a couple of weeks later; Yann congratulated him on the MuseSpark launch, and they have been publicly patching up on X. His framing: Yann is famously outspoken; that's just how he is.
- **"Too young / not technical."** The age line he's heard his whole career in Silicon Valley and barely registers anymore. The "not an engineer" line is straightforwardly wrong — *"once upon a time I was a software engineer in Silicon Valley"* — and his old math-olympiad background tends to be a reasonable predictor of competence in this domain, per Vance's own pattern-matching.
- **The bossing-people-around question.** His management philosophy is the Steve Jobs line: *"Most companies hire people and tell them what to do; we hire people for them to tell us what to do."* That, he says, is core to the entire thesis of TBD and MSL — they hired brilliant researchers and the job is to create the best environment for them to do the work of their careers.

The unifying register: a deliberate refusal to score points on any of it. He'd rather show than tell, and the showing is meant to be the next model.

## 6. MuseSpark as appetizer: clean stack, token efficiency, what's next (32:48)

This is the most substantive technical stretch of the interview, even though Wang is deliberately coy on specifics.

**What MuseSpark is, and isn't.** Over nine months Meta rebuilt the pre-training stack, the RL stack, the science layer, and much of the data work. MuseSpark is the *first data point on that rebuilt scaling ladder*. Wang's recurring metaphor: it's the **appetizer, not the entrée**. The next model on the ladder is the one he's actually excited about; the one after that, more so. The reason to ship MuseSpark anyway is that the entire program is built around **predictable scaling**, and a public data point matters even when it's early on the curve.

The scaling picture he sketches has multiple axes, all of which they claim to see scale predictably: pre-training, RL, test-time, and — newer and exciting to them — **multi-agent scaling** (the "content planning mode" Vance had been playing with the night before, in which roughly sixteen agents collaborate).

**The pleasant surprise: token efficiency.** Across artificial-analysis-style benchmarks, MuseSpark hits similar results with markedly fewer tokens than peer models. Wang's hypothesis is that this is a payoff from **building a clean stack from scratch**, the right way, by people who know how. If parts of a stack are subtly inefficient, the patch ends up being *let the model think longer* — and you see that downstream as token bloat. A clean stack avoids that tax. He's careful not to claim certainty about other labs' internals, but the implication is clear: token-efficiency is downstream of stack hygiene, and they expect it to keep paying off as they scale.

**What MuseSpark is good at vs. not yet.** It is *much* better than expected end-to-end, with some emergent behaviours that surprised them — particularly in **agentic visual coding**: producing real websites and games, an interaction of strong agentic ability with strong multimodality. Where it is *not* yet competitive is **agentic coding** writ large — that's a target for the next model.

**Why no frontier model yet, and when.** The one-word answer is *scaling*. They have predictability; they know what the next size up will deliver. Timing is "coming months." Vance notes that lands around a year into the whole effort, which lines up with Wang's claim that the front-loaded period was rebuilding foundations, and what comes now is *fast scaling mode*.

**Where the long-term product vision lands.** Wang loops MuseSpark's vision strength into the **Ray-Ban Metas** — millions of units sold, devices that "fade into the background" and capture context across your day. The endgame he describes is **a constellation of devices** powering a personal superintelligence: sees what you see, hears what you hear, proactively does research and takes actions, lives in the seams of the day instead of in a chat box.

Both hosts then concede an awkward truth: they themselves run their lives in WhatsApp and barely noticed the Meta-AI button there. Wang's explanation is that the integration was deliberately held back — *"we knew we needed to have great models and great products before we really pushed for tighter integration across the entire ecosystem."* Now that the models are landing, the knitting-together phase begins (analogous to what Gemini has done across Google over the last few years).

Vance lingers on a structural question that goes wider than Meta: this is a competition unlike any he's seen — OpenAI dominant in the consumer chatbot, Anthropic dominant in coding/business via Claude Code, Google distributing through services, X a wild card, Meta with billions of users but no settled consumer AI brand. Will it consolidate the way Word or the browser wars did? Wang's answer is **it's too early**. A year ago everyone would have said ChatGPT had already won consumer; then Claude Code became the fastest-growing business anyone has ever seen and overtook OpenAI's revenue, while Gemini ate meaningful consumer share. His read of that pattern: each new level of model capability **unlocks an entirely new form factor**, and the next wave will be bigger than the last. There is no endgame yet — and a lot of unbuilt categories are still ahead.

## 7. Why everyone hates AI, and the small-business agent thesis (48:47)

Robison frames it bluntly: she's in her 20s, not just in tech, and sees crazy AI-hating stuff on Instagram stories all the time. The sentiment is in the toilet — how do you square that with billions of users and the buttons you're shipping?

Wang concedes the sentiment is "very low, to say the least," and gives a single-sentence diagnosis: **AI has not yet demonstrated, in a visceral way, that it is a tool for personal empowerment.** For *developers* it has — Claude Code-style tooling has changed their lives, they can build whole projects in a weekend, sentiment is mixed-to-positive — *because* developers can now do things they couldn't. That moment hasn't happened for everyone else, and it hasn't happened for small-business owners.

That gap becomes Meta's strategic opening. The thing Meta uniquely has is the substrate: billions of consumers and *hundreds of millions of small businesses* already on the platforms — many running their business off WhatsApp, Facebook pages, Instagram pages, Meta ads. If you can put a genuinely powerful agent into the hands of each of those users *and* each of those businesses, and then enable the agents to *collaborate*, you change how supply and demand actually meet. Wang adapts the Dario Amodei line — *"country of geniuses in a data center"* — into his own version: **"economy of agents in a data center."** The whole point of the small-business framing is that it isn't a side feature; it's the lever that lets him claim a destination only Meta can credibly reach.

Trust is the bottleneck the hosts press on, and Wang doesn't argue with it. The way back is to build products that are *genuinely amazing for the people who use them* and to move in lock-step with social permission — *people see that we care about how these things are deployed and that we're genuinely making their lives better.*

## 8. Open source, safety preparedness, and the Manus question (53:50)

**Open source.** Vance baits the question by noting Meta's clear historical win in this space (Llama, OPC) and that MuseSpark was not open-sourced. Wang's answer is calibrated: Meta will continue open-sourcing models — that hasn't changed — but the most powerful models now have to clear a safety bar before they can be released openly. Concretely, MuseSpark in testing tripped some of the safety guardrails Meta's **advanced AI scaling framework** is designed to catch — **bio/chem, cyber capabilities, and loss-of-control** — all of which are detailed in the published preparedness report. So MuseSpark in its current form is not suitable for open-sourcing, but a meeting earlier that very day was about reviewing progress on **open-sourceable variants**. More to share "in the coming months."

**Internal alignment on direction.** Asked about the New York Times-style "Alex and Zuck want frontier research; Bosworth and Cox want products; Meta has to serve billions cheaply" framing, Wang flatly disputes the premise that there is meaningful disagreement, with a one-liner: *"the bar for journalistic reporting at major outlets is… the line between gossip and reporting is remarkably thin."* They debate points deeply but the goals are aligned: build the best models, integrate them into the business, ship products and services worthy of consumers and small businesses. There is, he says, no "major beef."

**Manus.** Vance presses on the squareness of two facts: Wang spent considerable time in DC flagging the China threat in AI, then Meta did a deal with **Manus**, a Chinese-origin startup (with Singapore footprint to create some distance). Wang can't discuss specifics — pretty clearly because there are still active negotiations, and *"if you can't comment, that means there's still machinations at play"* is Vance's read, undisputed. What he *will* say is principled: you have to separate *the state* from *the people*. His own parents are from China; many of the most talented people he gets to work with are Chinese — based in Singapore, the US, or elsewhere — and that is *independent* from his geopolitical views on the CCP and the PLA. He calls out a tendency in Silicon Valley (and especially on X) to flatten this into "China = bad" without the nuance, and rejects it.

**On the broader national-security stance.** Wang stands by the full-page AI-and-war ad he ran while at Scale: at that moment it was important for the US government to internalise that AI would be a *step change* for national security and for defending the country. The CCP and PLA have always taken AI extremely seriously; the US needed to catch up, and the events since — Mythos, among others — have, by his read, vindicated that case. He believes the US government today is, in fact, taking AI very seriously on national-security grounds.

**Are Anthropic over-doomers?** A complicated question, he says, but the *core message* is fair: these models are already very capable, will keep getting more so, and the upside is enormous (health superintelligence is one of his own examples) — *and* the risks deserve serious treatment.

## 9. Robots, mangoes, and health superintelligence with CZI (1:04:59)

**Why a humanoid-robotics acquisition (Assured Robot Intelligence / ARI).** ARI doesn't make hardware; it builds AI for various hardware targets. The strategic argument: if you take superintelligence and short timelines seriously, then **physical superintelligence is the natural next continuum** after digital superintelligence. Robotic intelligence also benefits from scaling in the same way models do — so given that Meta is already building compute infrastructure at the scale required, *not* applying it to world models and physical intelligence would be a waste. The application surface is huge: accelerating scientific discovery, accelerating goods manufacturing, and the more local "what does it look like for robots to make all of our lives way easier."

When Robison asks the obvious question — given the metaverse track record, *what makes you guys right to bring humanoids from Meta into the world?* — Wang's answer is non-defensive: you could be so scarred you don't get out of bed in the morning, *or* you can be excited about the technology, take real care with how you deploy it, and trust that if you build great products thoughtfully, people will be excited about them.

**Mangoes.** A rapid-fire detour. The "mango model" rumour mill: alive and well, he confirms cheerfully, and notes that the rumour churn around MSL is wildly outsized relative to what other labs get — *"we get a fraction that the other labs get; I have a lot of empathy for the drama of it all."* On **John Carmack** (back-of-envelope: ex-Meta, current AI effort, both Friedman and Gross were big early backers): Wang doesn't know what Carmack is currently doing — *"I don't know if anyone really knows what he's doing"* — but respects him as "one of the goat programmers." (Read: no band-getting-back-together announcement here.)

**Health superintelligence with CZI.** This is the longest of the rapid-fire threads because it's clearly important to him. MuseSpark scored very well on health benchmarks. Meta will be collaborating closely with the **Chan Zuckerberg Initiative** on it. Wang's strategic argument for why this is uniquely Meta's lane: the world stands to gain enormously from **equal access** to powerful health AI, and Meta uniquely can deliver that to billions of people because billions of people already use Meta products every day. Distribution is the moat.

## 10. Sci-fi, BCI, model welfare, and paradise on earth (1:11:08)

The hosts close on Wang's worldview because, as Vance puts it, he's been a bit of a mystery — they know where Dario stands, where Elon stands, where Sam stands, where Demis stands; what does Alex actually believe?

**Differentiation as a frontier lab.** Wang's first move is to refuse to claim a singular technical edge — *"you always want to show not tell"* — but he is excited about what is currently cooking and expects the next models to be state-of-the-art in the areas they care most about.

**Safety as table stakes.** He is firm on the philosophical baseline: *there is no such thing as building superintelligence without being very thoughtful about its safety risks*. The published MuseSpark preparedness report — more detailed than Meta has historically issued — is his existence proof.

**Where Meta specifically aims.** Personal superintelligence, deployed widely; democratised access; an era of *human abundance* in which augmented people accomplish more than any human ever has, alongside an agent economy making large strides on scientific discovery and health. *"How can we build paradise on earth? Superintelligence is a key milestone to get there."*

**Model welfare.** "Some people may kill me for mentioning this," but he flags it explicitly: *is it important for us to treat models well? Do models have moral weight?* He doesn't deliver a verdict — but he argues it changes day-to-day behaviour, and that nobody is talking about it nearly enough given how much of our work now lives with these systems. The grounding analogy: in a world where most humans care about how we treat plants, animals, and certainly other humans, it makes sense to also be thoughtful about how we treat the models. There is, he notes, real ongoing research into measuring the subjective experience of models (e.g. Eleos AI).

**Sci-fi and BCI.** Asked whether he's a sci-fi-pilled, Neuralink-believing transhumanist (with Vance flagging the genuine surprise that this is also a country-music-loving woods-walker who reads sci-fi for fun), Wang lays out which technologies he sees as critical-path for humanity over the long run: **superintelligence, robotics, brain–computer interfaces**, and underlying those, *energy, compute, and robots* as the inputs that scale to infinity into the future. He agrees Elon is more aggressively all-in on robotics/energy/BCI than anyone else — but where he disagrees with Elon is on **the role of research**: building superintelligence is *fundamentally a research activity*; on some level you are in the fog of war of knowledge, doing experiments to poke and prod what super-intelligence would even mean. Sequencing and milestones matter.

One concrete piece of evidence from FAIR's work he highlights: **Tribe B2**, a foundation-model effort for *brain prediction*. The cool result is **zero-shot generalisation** — without knowing who you are or having any data about your specific brain, the model can do a reasonable job of predicting how your brain would respond to various images, videos, or audio. That's not BCI hardware, but it's the modelling layer the field needs.
