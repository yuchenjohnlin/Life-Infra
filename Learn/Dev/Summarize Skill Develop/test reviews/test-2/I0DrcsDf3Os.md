## 1. A childhood shaped by math and early investment thinking (02:33)

Weng solved math problems faster than anyone in his class — not because he learned quickly, but because once he understood something, he built mental shortcuts that bypassed step-by-step reasoning. He calls this "System 1" processing: see the problem, see the answer, no intermediate thought required. Yet he describes himself as a slow learner who needs two to three times as long as his peers to absorb new material. The resolution: he needs more time to build his knowledge tree, but once built, he creates direct links — shortcuts — that let him skip the whole chain.

This self-awareness drove a distinctive habit: investing in the future. In middle school, he began teaching himself high school math. No one told him to. He reasoned that time spent on easy drill problems was wasted compared to learning something useful for later. By 8th grade he had finished the high school curriculum; by 9th grade he was studying calculus. His parents were hands-off — they didn't particularly care how he studied.

The motivation was internal. An initial reward from being good at math, compounded through positive feedback: every time he leveled up a skill, he got another hit of satisfaction, which pulled him further. He tried basketball — no positive feedback, got beaten by his roommates. He tried taekwondo — got crushed in sparring. Math was the domain where the reward loop was strongest, so that's where the investment went. It's the same reinforcement learning dynamic he would later study professionally.

## 2. Computer competitions and the high-stakes bet on Tsinghua (08:10)

Weng started programming in 7th grade through a private school's coding interest class — his parents thought it might suit him. High school brought serious competitive programming (OI). The motivation was largely pragmatic: for students outside Beijing, competitive programming was one of the few realistic paths to Tsinghua or Peking University. Regular Gaokao admission was, as Weng puts it, "impossibly hard."

His trajectory was rocky. He had also done math competitions but hit a ceiling at the provincial selection level — his school lacked the foundation, and he'd started too late for the highest tier. So he committed to OI. The NOIP (provincial round) was comfortable thanks to years of practice. But the provincial selection was a different story: in his first year, he could barely solve anything. In his second, he scraped through with heuristic methods — on one problem, minimum dual-circle covering, he scored 70 points, briefly the highest in the room. He made the Fujian provincial team as the last qualifier.

At the Tsinghua summer camp he earned a conditional offer: 60 points off the Gaokao, or admission at the first-tier cutoff if he won a silver medal at NOI. He won neither gold nor silver — only a bronze, the sole bronze from Fujian that year. This left him with only the 60-point reduction, a gamble given the time he'd lost from regular coursework. The alternative was a safer offer from Shanghai Jiao Tong: admission at the first-tier cutoff, no conditions. His family encouraged him to take the Tsinghua bet.

Even during the Gaokao prep year, he couldn't stop coding. He developed a practice of typing code directly on an iPad in Safari — no compiler, no syntax highlighting — and submitting raw, uncompiled code to online judges. This trained him to write bug-free programs through pure mental execution and forced a complete mental model of the problem before writing a single line.

## 3. Open-sourcing homework to break information asymmetry (16:02)

The first memory that surfaces when Weng thinks about Tsinghua is not a class or a professor — it's that he open-sourced all his coursework. He collected past exams, legacy materials, and his own homework, and published everything without copyright issues on GitHub. Some senior students opposed this.

Weng did it to break information asymmetry. At Tsinghua, knowing where to find past exams and study materials is a survival advantage — the kind of advantage that comes from social networks rather than ability. Weng believed everyone deserved equal access. Many capable students, he observed, spent ten or twenty hours stuck on assignments they could have understood quickly with the right reference, yet didn't dare ask teaching assistants. By open-sourcing his own solutions, he aimed to give future students more time to do what they actually wanted to do, instead of what he calls "life-wasting" busywork.

The repository became one of the most widely used resources in the CS department. He jokes: if you grab a random CS underclassman at Tsinghua and ask whether they know the person who donated the new building, the answer is no. Ask whether they know Weng Jiayi, and the answer is yes — because "everyone survived on my homework."

## 4. Stumbling into reinforcement learning (19:23)

Weng entered research in his sophomore year through a program called the Academic Rising Star Plan. A senior student recommended three professors: Zhu Jun, Tang Jie, and Cui Peng. Weng assumed the ordering reflected prestige and chose first — Zhu Jun. Three research directions were offered: Bayesian methods, GANs, and reinforcement learning. Weng actually wanted GANs — he was fascinated by image generation — but he didn't know which label corresponded to which topic. He picked RL by accident.

The first project was VizDoom: training a neural network to navigate a 1990s game, killing monsters, collecting health packs, and reaching an exit. The team won the competition. But Weng did not enjoy the research. The environment was too narrow. The work was pure overfitting — relying on heuristics and hyperparameter tuning that he estimates was ten to a hundred times harder than computer vision. Changing the algorithm rarely mattered; the real challenge was preventing training from collapsing. Everything was "alchemy."

This frustration became productive. Weng realized he was better at — and more interested in — the supporting infrastructure than the research itself. He could restructure code, improve user experience, make experiments run more smoothly. But the act of tuning hyperparameters provoked, in his words, a physical aversion. So he consciously shifted his energy toward building RL infrastructure that would let others do the research more efficiently: the idea that would eventually become Tianshou.

Alongside RL, Weng explored two other interests. He was deeply drawn to computer graphics — inspired by the film *TRON: Legacy*, he dreamed of building virtual worlds and took a graphics course where he rendered a noise-free 16K image, a first at the time, earning one of only two A+ grades. He also dabbled in network security, finding bugs in Tsinghua's campus systems (including a one-cent transcript download exploit that he reported to the administration). But he ultimately decided that splitting focus across fields was incompatible with serious research, and stayed with RL.

## 5. Summer research with Bengio and the limits of pre-scale NLP (28:00)

For his junior-year summer research, Weng's advisor connected him with Yoshua Bengio at Mila — the application had gone out before Bengio's Turing Award announcement in early 2019, so timing was fortunate. The assignment was to build something resembling what we now call Mixture of Experts: a router that selects different paths through a Transformer. The irony: Weng was recruited as an RL researcher but was given a pure NLP task. He was a beginner at NLP and Transformers, and by his own admission a slow learner. The concept of MoE didn't exist yet in its current form; nobody knew how to make it work.

The project produced no meaningful results. Looking back, Weng sees the failure as structural rather than personal: the compute was insufficient (a few GPUs for one intern), the MoE concept wasn't understood, and even with today's knowledge, the engineering capacity available couldn't have produced a working system at meaningful scale. The method was directionally correct — but being right about the direction doesn't help when you can't walk far enough.

The experience left Weng with two impressions of pre-ChatGPT NLP. First, the field's tasks were too fragmented — too many narrow benchmarks, too little unification. Second, many people were already asking how to apply RL to language model training, but nobody had solved the stability problem: a randomly initialized Transformer would simply collapse under RL training. The eventual answer, it turned out, required a "pure" environment (text-only) and a strong reward signal — conditions that wouldn't be met until later. At the time, both the context and the compute were missing.

## 6. Application setbacks and escaping fixed evaluation systems (32:47)

The graduate application season hit hard. Weng applied for PhD programs but received only Master's offers — including CMU. Surrounded by peers who had gone to Stanford and CMU for summer research and returned with first-author papers, the implicit Tsinghua hierarchy weighed on him: PhD is better than Master's. It's the same chain as GPA — a system so pervasive that even people who intellectually reject it feel its pull while immersed in it.

The disappointment was real, but it catalyzed a philosophical shift that had been building. Weng had already been questioning conventional evaluation systems. GPA, for instance, he treated with calculated minimalism: figure out the minimum score needed for his target threshold — say, 87 for a B+ — invest exactly that much effort, and refuse to spend one more minute chasing one more point. If the current grade was already enough, he'd skip exam prep entirely.

His advisor, Su Hang, offered an alternative framework for evaluating a computer science career: papers, competition results, and GitHub stars in the triple digits. This reframing was liberating. It pointed toward a space — the open-source community — where Weng could differentiate himself in ways that no GPA could capture. The real question, he realized, is what the demand side values. If a company is hiring, they care about relevant experience, not transcripts. A new grad with matching experience can outweigh years of seniority.

COVID complicated everything further. His CMU Master's offer was for fall 2020. By the time consulates reopened, he'd been stuck in China for months. Rather than obsessing over geopolitics, he turned inward: writing Tianshou and building tuixue.online — projects born from the conviction that investing time in what he could control was better than worrying about what he couldn't.

## 7. Tianshou: an RL framework born from frustration (41:08)

By December 2019, Weng had accumulated enough experimental RL code to feel the pull of consolidation. In February 2020, he spent a month reading RLlib — Ray's flagship RL library — hoping to adapt it. The verdict: too complex, too many abstractions, nearly a hundred thousand lines of code, and he couldn't figure out where to make changes. He scrapped it and started from scratch.

The first version took two weeks. Each algorithm was about twenty lines of code — because if the abstractions are right, implementation is trivial. The key to Tianshou's quality, Weng argues, was that a single person maintained consistency from top to bottom. Large codebases rot because contributors don't share full context: assumptions fail to propagate, code gets duplicated in slightly different ways, and the system bloats. A solo developer with a good top-level design avoids all of this. Consistency, not cleverness, is what makes a project good.

This was not a career move. Weng had no intention of publishing a paper. He'd already checked the boxes of his alternative evaluation framework — he had papers, he had competition results — and he specifically wanted a "real" three-digit GitHub star count. Tianshou caught on because it addressed a genuine need: researchers wanted an RL framework that was short, understandable, and easy to modify. Its design ensured that if you needed to change something, there was exactly one place to change it — you couldn't accidentally modify the wrong thing.

After Weng joined OpenAI and could no longer maintain it, he transferred ownership to the community. Five years later, some code rot has set in — the successor's context inevitably diverges from the original designer's. But Weng considers this acceptable: as long as one person makes the final call, consistency is maintainable, even if imperfect.

## 8. tuixue.online and the logic of impact (48:07)

tuixue.online was born from the same impulse as everything else Weng built: he had a need, couldn't find a tool, and wrote one himself. During COVID, US consulates opened and closed unpredictably, and Weng needed real-time visa appointment availability. He wrote a scraper, open-sourced it, and made it free. The first version was barely a system — it updated twice a day, manually. Even so, demand was massive. The site accumulated over ten million clicks before eventually shutting down when COVID subsided and the State Department changed its website structure.

Both Tianshou and tuixue were non-profit, post-application projects — Weng had nothing to gain from them professionally. He calls them "charity" and means it literally: completely non-profit, done because someone needed it. The technology is irrelevant; tuixue was a few lines of PHP. What matters is identifying a real need and serving it.

The deeper question is why. Weng traces the impulse to a thought that appeared in his mind during high school — he describes it as an idea that simply "popped into his head," possibly (he speculates only half-jokingly) sent backward from his future self. The thought: if life is a game, your final score is the number of people who remember your name when you die.

This isn't fame for its own sake. Weng distinguishes between celebrity attached to a position — sitting in a powerful chair — and recognition earned by genuinely helping people. He wants the kind of impact where people voluntarily give a thumbs-up: GitHub stars, tuixue clicks, the quiet knowledge that everyone in the CS department survived on his homework. The interviewer spots an interesting tension: Weng rejects external evaluation systems (GPA, PhD prestige) but replaces them with another external measure — how many people value his work. Weng's response is that this is his personal standard, not one he'd impose on others, and he's not enslaved by it. He's gone stretches without open-source projects and doesn't feel troubled. Besides, OpenAI's models serve as impact at a scale that dwarfs anything he could open-source individually.

## 9. Choosing OpenAI over DeepSeek (56:21)

At CMU, Weng initially approached the job search casually — eighteen applications, only two offers: Google and OctoML (Tianqi Chen's company). Google seemed boring, a big company where he'd work on things he didn't care about. He refined his approach and more offers came: Quantfang (which would become DeepSeek) for RL infra, NVIDIA for RL systems, and eventually OpenAI. FAIR was in the picture but rejected him on procedural grounds.

In hindsight, the core decision was DeepSeek vs. OpenAI — before either had become what they are today. Without OpenAI, Weng says he would have chosen Quantfang. What tipped the scale: OpenAI and DeepMind were the two best RL research labs in the world, and Weng wanted to experience how cutting-edge research was conducted at industrial scale — with methodology and resources, not the ad-hoc "small workshop" style of academic labs where a few PhDs hand-assemble something without clear process.

The interview with John Schulman was pivotal. Schulman gave Weng an open-ended, end-to-end coding problem with a three-hour limit. Weng finished in two hours. A bug appeared during the demo, which he fixed live. Schulman valued Weng's GitHub portfolio — it aligned with his own evaluation criteria for what makes a strong candidate. Schulman had tested this interview question on exactly two people: Weng and Andrey Mishchenko (who would later work on Codex). Both passed. When Schulman left OpenAI, Weng shut his laptop and did nothing for the rest of the afternoon.

## 10. Why a PhD is a waste for industry AI (1:01:54)

Weng never considered a PhD. His reasoning is blunt: if your goal is industry, a PhD wastes years of your life. A Master's is sufficient as a stepping stone if you accumulate enough differentiation — citations, distinctive projects, engineering skill that lets you compete with PhD candidates. The key is understanding what the hiring company actually needs, then optimizing for that.

A colleague at OpenAI — also an RL PhD who built a well-known RL framework — crystallized the deeper insight: teaching a researcher to do good engineering is far harder than teaching an engineer to do good research. In the current era, engineering ability dominates. Frontier research is won not by algorithmic novelty but by infra correctness and iteration speed. Ideas are cheap — discuss with experienced people, ideas emerge. What matters is how many valid ideas you can verify per unit time, on correct infrastructure, with correct results. Research intuition comes from time spent in the field; after three years of industry work, anyone develops it. The bottleneck is execution.

Every lab's infrastructure has bugs. Whoever fixes more bugs trains better models. Weng is willing to speculate that Llama's underperformance relative to GPT might simply be an infra quality gap.

This led to a personal optimization: maximize the number of times his name appears on the OpenAI blog. The strategy: build infra that everyone uses, not individual research that doesn't scale. Since he built OpenAI's entire post-training RL infrastructure, his name appears on every major model release. And the position has high ecological value — RL sits at the top of the infra stack, not at the bottom where a data loader or storage layer would yield less visibility.

For students today considering an AI PhD, Weng's advice is direct: skip it and enter industry early. You don't know what paradigm shifts will happen during a five-year PhD. AI labs currently need infra people more than researchers. If your goal is to join an AI lab, figure out what they're actually hiring for — right now, the answer is infra — and build those skills, even without a PhD.

## 11. ChatGPT: an accidental blockbuster (1:13:13)

When Weng joined OpenAI in July 2022, there was no "post-training" as a concept — the team was simply called "RL." ChatGPT was not the company's main project. Only John Schulman's small RL team was working on it, building on top of the WebGPT project, which had tried to use GPT-3.5 for web browsing but fallen back to a simpler goal: making conversational interaction work through instruction-following via RLHF. The contributor list on the ChatGPT blog post tells the story: John Schulman first, then about twelve names from his team.

Weng used the model internally before launch and was not particularly impressed — a talking model that could sometimes help with code, but with obvious limitations. The gradual exposure flattened any sense of revelation. The original launch plan was modest: release it, see if it could collect real-world user data, maybe attract ten to twenty thousand users. If interest faded after five days, shut it down. The actual trajectory was exponential. After launch on November 30, 2022, Weng was at NeurIPS when he noticed everyone around him discussing ChatGPT. The servers crashed repeatedly — the same pattern as tuixue during visa season: organic virality, users as self-propagating marketing, infrastructure buckling under demand nobody had planned for.

## 12. Talent density and organizational architecture at OpenAI (1:20:52)

Weng's first impression of OpenAI was "a large-scale lab" — less methodologically rigorous than he expected, but populated with people who had strong research intuition and could point the way forward. The real transformation came when Barret Zoph, Luke Metz, and Liam Fedus joined from Google and brought what Weng calls "Google's advanced productivity." The core concept: a chart showing that iterations per unit time correlate linearly with success rate. More attempts, more wins — the same trial-and-error logic as RL itself. The philosophical shift was: don't reach for genius ideas first; get the infra right so you can go from thirty iterations per week to three hundred.

Sam Altman's principle — that in a team of extremely high talent density, mediocre performance is intolerable — resonates with Weng. High density creates emergent capabilities that a team of average performers cannot produce. But maintaining this as OpenAI scaled from 280 people (when Weng joined) to 3,000+ is a different challenge.

The key to organizational effectiveness, Weng argues, is information flow: decisions from leadership reaching execution without distortion, and progress from the ground reaching leadership without loss. Sam Altman had a dedicated research assistant tracking internal technical progress; Greg Brockman personally participated in most infrastructure work. Managing a company, Weng observes, is like managing a codebase: consistency is everything. When the body moves but the feet don't, you have a problem.

## 13. Post-training RL and the RLHF breakthroughs of 2022 (1:24:09)

A revealing timeline detail: the PPO pipeline on GPT-3.5 used the old infrastructure and was never fully tuned. Weng's first successful PPO run was on GPT-4, using the new infrastructure that came online around August 2022. GPT-4 existed internally before ChatGPT launched, and RLHF was debugged on GPT-4 first, then applied back to GPT-3.5.

The central challenge of RLHF in 2022 was evaluation: nobody knew what "good" looked like. A single reward signal could saturate — reward hacking — where the reward metric plateaus while actual human-perceived quality first rises, then falls. Selecting the right checkpoint was an art form with no reliable automated answer. The team built sampling-based evaluations across multiple benchmarks, but even those yielded high-variance, noisy numbers that couldn't reliably distinguish between checkpoints.

The ultimate arbiter was always human evaluation: pull the model down, interact with it, have multiple people try it, and vote. RLHF's evaluation method, in the end, was human feedback — the same "HF" that the technique is named after. There was no shortcut around this in 2022, and the constraint shaped the entire development process.

## 14. Industrial-scale RL infra: the real bottleneck in AI (1:27:01)

The gap between academic and industrial RL infra is enormous. In toy environments — Atari, MuJoCo — the bottleneck is the environment: the model is small, training and action sampling are cheap. In LLM-scale RL, the relationship inverts. The environment is trivial (a text prompt, taking microseconds), but the model is massive: a single inference-training loop can take hundreds or thousands of seconds. Scaling up — using more GPUs, increasing throughput — is the core challenge, requiring deep, end-to-end optimization that reaches into model inference implementation details.

This is an intersection that almost nobody is trained for: you need to understand RL, ML systems, and large-model inference simultaneously. The position is highly educational, Weng notes, but also brutal. During one intense period, he worked from waking to sleeping roughly six days a week and ended up in the emergency room with severe headaches. The doctor found nothing wrong. He now runs 3km twice a week — a dramatic change from his Tsinghua days when he couldn't pass the 3km PE test.

The recognition that iteration speed matters more than anything else extends forward. Weng frames the future of LLM development as a question of throughput: how many bugs can you fix per unit time, and how many correct iterations can you run? If you fix all the bugs, you might not need to change the algorithm at all. His team is now building the next-generation OpenAI RL infra from scratch — a full rewrite, because three years of accumulated technical debt mirror the same code-rot pattern he saw in RLlib and even in Tianshou after he left. Multiple people contributing without shared context leads to inconsistency, and inconsistency leads to bloat.

This rewrite is motivated partly by internal alarm: DeepSeek's claimed iteration speed made OpenAI realize its own infra cycle time was not competitive. The goal is to give researchers fundamentally faster iteration — because in a world where ideas are cheap and available, the winner is whoever can verify more ideas per unit time on correct infrastructure.

## 15. Can current methods reach AGI? (1:36:30)

AGI definitions at OpenAI are notoriously personal — grab fifteen people, get twenty definitions. Weng's: if a system can complete 80-90% of the tasks he personally considers meaningful, it qualifies. By that standard, we're not there. He still wouldn't trust an AI to modify his infra code — AI infrastructure is so far out of distribution relative to training data that it's essentially unmapped territory. The verification feedback loops are too long and too expensive.

When Strawberry (o1) became available internally, many at OpenAI initially felt their jobs were about to be automated. The running joke was "let's just write terrible code — Strawberry will clean it up later." A year or two on, the technical debt remains. Everyone overreacted. Real progress is incremental, not the sudden displacement people imagined.

The current frontier hasn't been fully exploited. There is still headroom in large-scale RL experimentation before anyone can declare the current paradigm has hit its ceiling. And the infrastructure still has bugs, which means fixing those bugs alone — without any algorithmic innovation — might unlock significant performance gains. You can't predict what comes next from where things stand now; new paradigms for both pre-training and RL are possible.

## 16. Is OpenAI still open? (1:38:34)

Weng remains a lover of open source, but he accepts the trade-off. A company that open-sources its best model immediately hands the lead to competitors who can fine-tune it and close-source the result. Not everyone shares OpenAI's mission — some just want to make money — and that asymmetry makes unilateral openness a game-theoretic trap. OpenAI needs revenue to survive, and survival enables continued research toward AGI.

The "open" in OpenAI, in Weng's reading, should be understood not as openness to other AI companies, but as openness to ordinary people: free-tier ChatGPT, voice mode, accessible products that let non-technical users benefit from frontier technology. A raw model weight dump doesn't help a user who doesn't know what to do with it.

John Schulman once asked Weng whether they should open-source the RL infra. Weng's instinct was no — for the company's sake — though he would "of course" be happy to do it if resources were unlimited. DeepSeek's open-weights release forced an internal reassessment. The calculus depends on timeline: if AGI is a sprint away, keeping the lead closed makes sense. If the race is longer, community feedback could accelerate progress. But in the current competitive landscape, with not everyone aligned to the same goals, the trade-off favors caution.

## 17. Sam Altman's firing, talent departures, and the AI race (1:43:30)

The November 2023 board crisis, as seen from inside OpenAI, was simple: the board — with Ilya Sutskever among them — didn't trust Sam Altman and voted to remove him. The rank and file were blindsided; the board had operated without transparency toward employees. Internet speculation about "what Ilya saw" was, in Weng's account, rumor and fabrication. The official investigation report told the accurate story: it was a trust breakdown, not a safety alarm.

When employees had to choose sides, most backed Altman — not from personal loyalty but from pragmatic assessment. A pure technologist couldn't navigate the commercial, political, and fundraising dimensions required for the AGI mission. AGI isn't only a technical problem: you need funding, compute, partnerships, and the ability to persuade people to invest. Sam's value is as an identity — a social interface that AI can't yet replicate, because convincing humans to commit resources requires human-to-human persuasion.

On talent departures, Weng is philosophical: a healthy organization is one where everyone is replaceable. As long as it can cultivate new talent — "blood-making capacity, like stem cells" — departures are survivable. The converse also holds: if nobody at OpenAI is irreplaceable, then what OpenAI does isn't impossibly hard to replicate. There's no black magic, just the basics done well.

The internal reaction to external competition is selective. Benchmark scores on LMSys leaderboards don't cause anxiety — OpenAI hasn't specifically optimized for them in a long time. What alarmed them was DeepSeek's claimed iteration speed. That's the metric that actually determines who builds the best model. OpenAI, Weng admits, is not the fastest-iterating organization in the world — startups with smaller codebases and lower communication overhead iterate faster. Every organization at scale faces this: context sharing degrades, infra becomes inconsistent, structures bloat. The theoretical solution: an agent with infinite context length, serving as a CEO that maintains perfect organizational coherence. It might be the most urgent use case for AGI — running the companies that build AGI.

## 18. Determinism, meaning, and investing in the unknown future (1:52:48)

Weng is a hard determinist. The universe is fully predetermined — not random, not probabilistic at the macro level, just deterministic causation from the Big Bang onward. He's tried repeatedly to falsify this view and failed. If he could build one AI system to solve one world problem, it would be one that predicts the future — not the trajectory of a falling cup, but the trajectory of lives, geopolitics, everything. He immediately adds that such a system would be catastrophic: it would collapse all value systems. The best response to its creation might be to destroy it. The contradiction doesn't trouble him — he's driven by the same curiosity that leads physicists to probe foundations.

He offers a speculative resolution to the tension between determinism and the experience of agency: time may not be linear in higher dimensions. His high-school epiphany about impact — the idea that simply appeared in his mind — might have been his future self sending information backward, like Murphy's bookshelf in *Interstellar*. He acknowledges this sounds like nonsense. He's fine with that. The practical response to determinism, he says, is to forget about it and live as if choices matter.

On entrepreneurship, he's interested but hasn't found the right idea. He'd want a product-oriented venture built around user need — like Tianshou or tuixue — where the technology is secondary. Even a PHP scraper can serve millions if the need is real.

In ten years, he hopes his future self will have the resources and freedom to pursue whatever matters to him then. He's investing in the future again — the same logic from middle school. But he's in a rare period of uncertainty. The things he once wanted — RL infra, impact — now feel like they have a visible ceiling. AGI, he says, is a foregone conclusion. What remains is figuring out what genuinely matters. He thought he'd solved that question once. He hasn't.
