> A long-form podcast interview by Ryan Peterman (a software engineer who runs *The Peterman Pod* and the *developing.dev* newsletter) with David J. Malan, the Harvard professor who has been at the helm of CS50 — Harvard's introductory computer science course — for 19 years. Malan is the public face of CS50's transformation from a campus lecture into one of the most-watched MOOCs on the internet, and the conversation sits at the intersection of three things he is unusually positioned to discuss: how to lecture well, how universities should (and don't) share resources, and how AI is reshaping intro CS — both the curriculum and the cheating around it. Aimed at CS students, prospective students, and educators rather than at researchers; the level is reflective and pedagogical, not technical.

## TL;DR

A 19-year veteran of CS50 explains how the course became what it is, and why the things students worry about (Is C still worth learning? Will AI make programming useless? Should I even study CS?) are mostly the wrong worries.

- **Lecture engagement is theatricality in service of memory, not entertainment.** The phone-book demo, students sorting themselves on stage, lockers with hidden numbers — all of it exists so that *years later*, when the course has long faded into a firehose blur, the alumnus can still picture a specific room and a specific friend acting out bubble sort. Memorable moments are how dense material survives in memory.
- **The conscious choice to keep 3-hour lectures** instead of MOOC-style 3-minute chunks: bite-sized content drives engagement metrics but not educational outcomes, and online viewers can pause, rewind, and follow rabbit holes in ways the in-person classroom can't support.
- **Higher education has a massive inefficiency problem** — thousands of faculty redoing the same lectures and problem sets in parallel — and Malan has tried and largely failed to get even peer institutions to share courses across campuses, including during COVID when the case was easiest to make. He thinks the resistance is part school pride, part fear of being "put out of business."
- **C is taught not because students will use it, but because they should understand what's underneath.** Building your own hash table in week five and then collapsing it to a one-line Python dict in week six is the whole pedagogical point — the course is producing engineers, not coders.
- **On AI: the rubber duck at cs50.ai is deliberately less helpful than ChatGPT.** Academic dishonesty rates haven't actually risen (still the long-running 5–10%), but *prosecuting* cases is harder because there's no smoking-gun URL anymore. The bigger anxiety — "is programming about to be useless?" — Malan reframes: the course was never about Scratch or C or Python, it was always about problem-solving, and AI just removes the parts of coding that weren't fun anyway.
- **Two pieces of personal advice that recur throughout:** *calm down and explore more* (he wishes he'd taken Latin and theatre and archaeology earlier), and *time is a perfectly reasonable knob to turn* when something isn't clicking — take 24 weeks instead of 12, or try a different teacher, before concluding the field isn't for you.

## 1. From government major to CS50 instructor (0:00)

Malan didn't arrive at Harvard expecting to study computer science. He'd liked constitutional law in high school, so he came in concentrating in government and stayed heads-down on it for a year and a half. It took him until sophomore year to work up the nerve to follow some friends into CS50, then taught for a single visiting year by Brian Kernighan. He was hooked immediately — and not in the abstract: it was the first time in nineteen years that *homework was fun*, fun enough that he looked forward to Friday nights in his dorm working through problem sets. That, more than any career calculation, was the signal that he was supposed to study CS.

The opening detail — a photo of his first Hello World, marked minus two out of two — sets the running tone of the episode: the person who now runs the most-watched intro CS course in the world started by failing the first assignment over something stupid and easily fixable. The pivot wasn't a single decision so much as an accumulating one. He taught his first class — *Introduction to Computers and the Internet* — as a senior at Harvard's Extension School, the continuing-ed program for adults, in front of a room of about a hundred people, easily the youngest in the room at twenty-one. He wore suspenders that one time, trying to project being older. That taste of teaching, more than the CS itself, set the next decade in motion: a PhD at Harvard, and in 2007, when his own advisor became dean and CS50 had a sudden vacancy at the helm, he "somehow talked his way into the job." He was meant to be a one-year stopgap. That was nineteen years ago.

## 2. How CS50 went online, organically (3:27)

What the world now sees as a deliberately produced online course was, in Malan's telling, an evolution rather than a revolution. The first year he taught CS50 — spring 1999 — they filmed on VHS, with the camera operated by his own friends (who were also the teaching fellows), and there is allegedly still footage somewhere of a TF falling asleep mid-class with the camera running. The online presence began at the Extension School around 2000, when distance education was just becoming a thing and his mentor Henry Leitner offered him the chance to put the course online.

The path from there to a global MOOC ran through whichever technology happened to land next. When Apple released the iPod around 2003, the team figured out how to repackage their RealVideo/RealAudio streams as MP3s and publish them via an RSS feed on iTunes — initially for their own Extension School students, because streaming at the time required a constant internet connection (buffering, Malan notes, "hadn't really been invented yet"). When the video iPod arrived a year or two later, they steered into video. *Wired* eventually noticed they were among the most popular podcasts in their category, which surprised them — though they already knew something was happening from a more practical signal: their DreamHost free-tier accounts kept hitting bandwidth limits, so they'd open more free accounts and use DNS trickery to swap hosts inside the RSS file.

The decision to open the materials freely to the public was a touchy subject on campus, because Extension School students were paying for what others could now get for nothing. Malan's answer to that tension is a clean one and worth quoting directly: what people are paying for is the additional *support structure*, the credentialing, the transcript, the credit. The knowledge itself — "it does society no good to withhold that."

## 3. Memorable moments: theatricality as a memory aid (11:19)

When Peterman asks whether Malan thinks about "hooking" the online audience the way social-media creators do, Malan separates the two layers carefully. For *social* content — interviews, fair excerpts, the occasional meme — yes. For the lectures themselves, no. CS50 deliberately did not steer into the bite-sized 3- to 5-minute chunks that came into fashion in 2012 with Coursera and edX. Yes, there is data that short formats drive engagement; there is not data that they drive educational outcomes. CS50 stuck to roughly 3-hour lectures, on the reasoning that online video actually *solves* the in-person downside: you can pause, rewind, search, open another tab, chase a tangent. The classroom can't support any of that. The bargain he's offering students is "meet us halfway" — if the lecture takes you more than three hours to watch, that's fine, but the content isn't designed for scrolling.

How then do you hold attention for three hours? Not with pacing tricks. With **memorable moments**. The framing is unusual and worth spelling out: the bits Malan is famous for — tearing a phone book in half to demonstrate binary search, students physically sorting themselves on stage, gym lockers with hidden numbers behind their doors — are not designed primarily to keep eyes on the screen. They are designed to *latch onto memory*. The reasoning is that CS50 is the proverbial firehose, and most of it will inevitably blur. What survives, years after Harvard, is the visual: *my roommate was literally the one on stage doing bubble sort.* That hook into a specific memory becomes the handle by which the underlying concept can be retrieved. Alumni come back and tell him they still remember the phone book demo. The theatricality, in his phrase, is what brings material — CS or anything else — to life. (He notes the cultural cost too: the phone-book bit has gotten harder every year because students no longer know what a phone book is.)

## 4. Why dryness has no place in a lecture (14:25)

The obvious objection is the depth-versus-engagement trade-off. If you spend ten minutes on linear search and fifteen on binary search and have students physically acting things out on stage, you're necessarily moving through less material than the chalkboard professor calmly writing it all down. Malan grants the criticism fully — students with prior background do find the theatrical detours slow. CS50 does spend disproportionate time on cherry-picked topics that lend themselves to drama, and they've written about that explicit trade-off in a CS-education paper.

But he refuses the framing that dryness is the price of depth. His argument is more pointed: dryness has no real place in *most forms* of education at all, because if the lecture is just someone reciting words or writing them down non-interactively, "it could have been a book, it could have been an email." The justification for getting people into a room together is the part that *can't* be a book. That's where the energy comes from — and he traces his own energy frankly to "a place of insecurity": he doesn't want to be the one on stage in front of a bored audience. The high-energy delivery, then, is doing double duty: it makes students *want* to stay, and it offers them an honest look at why he himself fell in love with the field, so they can decide for themselves whether they feel the same pull.

## 5. The inefficiency of education, and why universities won't share (18:11)

This is the part of the conversation where Malan is most opinionated, and probably most frustrated. Peterman asks the obvious computer-scientist question: if online education has effectively zero marginal cost, and CS50 is freely available, why doesn't the field consolidate around a handful of the best courses from the best teachers? Why have thousands of faculty preparing essentially the same lectures, the same problem sets, the same grading, in parallel?

Malan agrees that the inefficiency is real and that the computer scientist in him "invariably wants to factor that out." He's not proposing one course to rule them all — healthy competition is good, and he'd guess dozens rather than thousands of intro courses would be the right number. But even modest gestures in this direction have been hard. CS50 has run jointly with Yale for ten years and now with Oxford's lifelong-learning group; both are still the exception. His most striking example is one of personal regret: during COVID, when *everything* was already online and there was every reason to swap resources across campuses, he could get no one at Harvard interested in offering one of their CS courses to Stanford or Yale or MIT students in exchange for, say, a digital-humanities course going the other way. The institutional appetite simply wasn't there.

When Peterman presses on what the actual blocker is, Malan's diagnosis is mostly cultural: school pride, and a fear of "putting ourselves out of business." He pushes back on the second concern directly — sharing materials is not a failure of an institution, it's an opportunity to do better with what each institution actually has. The buffet metaphor is his: education as a set of shared materials that each campus then makes its own, the way cross-campus collaboration is already routine in research but somehow "very fundamentally threatening" in teaching.

## 6. Why start with C (23:20)

This is the most pedagogically dense section of the interview, and it's worth following carefully because Malan's defense of C doubles as a defense of the whole CS50 worldview.

The premise of the question — "I'm going into full-stack web work, why am I writing C?" — Malan reframes immediately. The choice of C is not about future *use*, it's about future *understanding*. C strikes a near-unique balance: English-like syntax sitting just one step above assembly, a small standard library that forces students to build things themselves, and procedural constructs (loops, conditions, functions, variables, return values) that generalize cleanly to almost every other language they'll touch. By mid-semester — week five — students are building their own hash tables, singly and doubly linked lists, tries, trees, stacks, queues. Crucially, in C you can't just instantiate a hash table the way you can with Java or C++ STL: you have to build it.

His three reasons that doing this is pedagogically worth it, in his own ordering:

1. So you can later make informed decisions about how to design your own data structures.
2. So you can diagnose problems by reasoning from first principles about what could possibly go wrong — because you actually understand how the data is being stored and what the algorithms are doing to it.
3. As a scaffold to higher-level languages. The set piece here is the transition from week five to week six: that whole hash-table implementation, however many lines of code, gets collapsed in week six to a single line — `dict = {}` in Python. The contrast itself *is* the lesson.

He pre-empts the obvious objection — many courses teach intro programming in Python alone, including some of CS50's own audience-specific variants — but warns that Python-only students "never really get around to understanding what's going on underneath the hood." And this is where the line that animates the whole course comes out: CS50's goal is *not to output programmers, but engineers and educated citizens* who understand from first principles how technology works. C is the lever that makes that goal achievable in a semester.

The later coda is consistent. When Peterman brings up a YouTube video he'd watched titled "whatever you do, don't take CS50" — whose argument was that a full-stack engineer doesn't need any of this — Malan, half-joking that he doesn't want to start an internet fight, calls it absolutely the wrong mindset *especially* for a full-stack engineer (by definition, full stack means understanding the layers). The reformulation he offers is sharp: it's not that you don't need to *know* these things; it's that you won't need to *use* them. Knowing the underlying building blocks is what separates someone who can build the next thing from someone who can output what AI can already output.

## 7. The rubber duck, and what's left of cheating (31:51)

CS50 has a final lecture each year on AI — only an hour, deliberately broad, scheduled to coincide with family weekend so parents can attend. Its purpose is partly to introduce the field, partly to demystify the tools the students have been *already using all semester*: the virtual rubber duck at cs50.ai, built on OpenAI's APIs via Microsoft Azure.

What Malan finds worth designing carefully is the duck itself, not the lecture. The duck is **deliberately a less helpful version of ChatGPT** — tuned to CS50's syllabus and tuned, more importantly, to behave like a good tutor: lead you toward the solution without auto-completing your way through it. The technical lift to do this is much smaller in 2026 than it was in 2022 when they first rolled it out, but Malan defends the decision to ship a separate tool rather than just publish a system prompt for students to paste into ChatGPT. The reason is essentially behavioral: nobody will actually do the paste, the prompt will drift out of date, and most importantly there's no clean line for students to know they've crossed. With the duck-at-cs50.ai approach, the line is unambiguous: you can use the duck as much as you want, and going to ChatGPT or Gemini or Copilot is, by syllabus, an academically dishonest choice. The clarity of the boundary is the design goal.

The more practically interesting claim follows. **Cheating rates have not gone up.** CS50 has long disciplined between 5% and 10% of the student body each semester through its plagiarism-detection tooling (cross-comparing submissions against each other, against historical GitHub repos, against transcribed YouTube videos of past problem sets). That number is consistent with peer institutions' upper bounds and hasn't moved with AI. What *has* changed is the prosecution side: when AI generates the answer, there's no longer a smoking-gun URL to hand the honor council. Malan's response is that experience itself becomes the detector — they can usually tell when the work doesn't match the student's other submissions, or when the sophistication looks wrong, or when, classically, the AI has solved *last year's* problem set instead of this year's, a giveaway that the student didn't write it.

When Peterman observes that 5–10% feels like a lot, Malan agrees gently — twenty years ago he would have agreed strongly. After enough years, he's mostly relieved it isn't higher.

## 8. Should you still learn CS? (38:21)

This is the question the era keeps producing, and Malan has clearly thought about how to answer it without dismissing the anxiety. His core move is to refuse the framing — "I wouldn't worry about getting better at programming." The whole point of a course like CS50 has never been Scratch or C or Python or JavaScript as ends in themselves; those are pedagogical *vehicles* for problem-solving, and problem-solving is a life skill that survives the field. The repeated CS50 line — "the course is not about Scratch and C and Python and JavaScript" — is doing real work here.

He concedes the obvious: AI is already very good at generating code and only going to get better. But his framing of what AI takes is precise. AI takes the parts that were "never very fun to begin with" — he gives the example of unit tests, which can now be generated robustly and which he admits he himself has never enjoyed writing. What it doesn't take, at least not yet, is the design layer: thinking about the UX, the database schema, what data you'd want to collect from a business problem because you know you'll need to mine it later. That layer is still human.

His second argument is historical. He took CS50 in 1996 at the peak of the dot-com era, watched the bottom drop out, then watched the pendulum swing back. He thinks the world is only getting more technological, every business will keep needing more of it, and the current downturn — which he confirms is real on campus and visible in recruiting — will swing back too. He pulls AR/VR, blockchain, Google Glass into the same frame: each was hyped, each is still around in some form, and the VC world reliably overreacts in both directions before settling.

There's a candid moment in the middle where Malan describes his own current relationship with these tools. He'd been playing with Claude that morning to prototype something, and it returned a confident, 90%-correct solution that he had to talk it out of — pointing to the actual API docs to correct it. His takeaway: in *today's* environment, the tools empower the human, they don't replace; that's why the human stays in charge. He doesn't pretend to know what happens when 90% becomes 99% or 100%, but he points out that even in that world the underlying goal of CS50 — to produce a more educated citizenry who can teach themselves and reason algorithmically — is invariant under whatever the tools do.

## 9. College vs online education (45:24)

This is the shortest section but the one with the cleanest single answer. Peterman observes that Malan is uniquely positioned: he runs both the in-person Harvard class and the massively-open online version of the same course, and outcomes between the two populations are very different. So what is college actually for?

Malan's answer separates the value into two parts, both honest. **The value of Harvard specifically is the credentialing, the admissions filter, the networking, the life experiences — and academics, he says bluntly, are "sometimes to a fault secondary."** But on knowledge and learning outcomes and practical skills, he'd argue you get as good if not *better* an experience taking CS50 online, for purely technological reasons: you can pause, rewind, fast-forward, open multiple tabs, chase rabbit holes in ways the traditional classroom is not designed to support. It's an unusually direct concession from someone with skin in the game on both sides.

## 10. Pointers, metaphors, and the growth mindset (47:06)

Asked which concept students consistently struggle with most, Malan answers without hesitation: **pointers in C.** He gives a personal vignette — he remembers exactly where on campus the lightbulb finally went off for him, sitting in the back of a Harvard dining hall with his TF, when something the TF said made him realize, "Oh, it's just an address." It hadn't clicked despite many earlier explanations. Memory is just hard, especially when the computer itself is still an abstraction; going down a level requires new thinking, and it takes time.

His pedagogical advice for teaching anything hard is consistent with the rest of the course's philosophy: **metaphors and memorable moments.** The phone book for binary search, or the iOS contacts screenshot for the same algorithm. During COVID, when they couldn't have students physically on a stage, they worked with a local theatre to get seven actual stage doors with prop numbers behind them — physical doors as binary doors. Even for binary numerals he'll show students that 123 has a ones place, a tens place, a hundreds place; binary is exactly the same idea, just powers of two. The point isn't the cleverness of the metaphor — it's that the metaphor lets the student realize "Oh, that's all it is?", which puts them inside the conversation. The most arcane-seeming topics turn out to be within reach.

The conversation then naturally turns to growth versus fixed mindset. Peterman asks the hard form of the question: is there really someone who just *can't* learn CS, no matter how many times you explain arrays? Malan's answer is more nuanced than the standard growth-mindset script. If something isn't sinking in after a hundred attempts and it's not exciting you, then yes — that's empirical evidence the field isn't for you, and you should stop beating yourself up and find something else. But if you're considering giving up after two or ten or even forty-nine tries, you should keep going — and not by trying the same approach harder. Find a different class, a different professor, a different TA, a different book. The angle of attack matters more than effort.

The line he returns to is the operational one: **time is a perfectly reasonable knob to turn.** If you can't finish CS50 in twelve weeks, take twenty-four, or fifty-two. That alone, he thinks, puts the material within reach of vastly more people than the standard semester pace implies.

## 11. The future of CS50 (52:35)

The future of CS50, as Malan sees it, is much more about expansion of the surrounding ecosystem than reinvention of the core. The lecture style and the problem sets evolve every year — comparing fall 1996 to fall 2026, packaging and stylization are very different — but the conceptual backbone is remarkably stable. CS50 will keep being, at its core, an introductory course in problem-solving.

The expansion is happening around it. CS50 has been developing a fuller curriculum: courses students can take *after* CS50 via Open Courseware, gentler courses they can take *before* or even *instead of* CS50 depending on background. The near-term plan is to branch beyond CS — beginning with mathematics, in collaboration with Tom Crawford at Oxford, partly so students arriving at CS courses without the requisite math background have somewhere to go. Beyond math, STEM more broadly is on the roadmap, and longer term, working with humanities and arts colleagues to bring the same theatricality-and-memorable-moments approach to those subjects.

When Peterman names Khan Academy as a likely competitor, Malan welcomes it. Healthy competition is good for the field, and "the world only benefits from more educational resources." He distinguishes the styles: Khan Academy began with a single-instructor, handwritten aesthetic (Sal Khan teaching his niece); CS50 has always tried to keep a shared cast of characters, a shared aesthetic, a shared pedagogical and lecturing style across courses, so that taking *any* CS50 course feels recognizably part of the same ecosystem.

## 12. Regrets, books, and advice (55:56)

The closing section threads three of the uploader's chapters together — they're naturally one reflective movement at the end of the conversation.

**On regret**, Malan's biggest is personal and educational, not professional: he wishes someone had told him, or that he had known, to *calm down*. He came into Harvard with a literal sheet of paper listing all 32 courses he planned to take, focused on crossing requirements off the list, and didn't break out of that mold until very late — a senior-spring Latin course taken purely for fun, which retroactively unlocked aspects of English and Spanish he'd never mastered; a Dramatic Arts 101 survey class on theatre; an archaeology course audited in grad school that briefly made him wonder whether he should have done a PhD in archaeology instead. The lesson he tries to pass on to current students is to explore more, *especially* in fields where you expect to be less comfortable.

The professional regret is gentler and more specific: he's spent his whole career in academia and has never had what he half-jokingly calls "a real job." A recent sabbatical as a professor-in-residence at GitHub — sitting in on design chats, filing issues and feature requests, being the educational voice in product decisions about VS Code and GitHub Classroom — was so much fun that he wishes he'd done something like it earlier, or at least picked up consulting in parallel along the way.

**On books**, his actual favorite — and he gives this answer straight — is *The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy*. (Or the terminal-based text adventure version of it, which he played growing up.) For technology specifically, CS50 has officially stopped recommending books for years, partly in keeping with the course's commitment to openness and to not requiring students to spend money. Unofficially, his favorites are the brilliantly illustrated *How Computers Work* and *How the Internet Works* (now somewhat outdated), and for students who want to go deeper into low-level C, *Hacker's Delight* — a hardcover full of bit-twiddling tricks and performance hacks. He also has unexpectedly warm words for the old *For Dummies* and *Complete Idiot's Guide* lines: the names are awkward, but the books are written for genuinely broad audiences, in contrast to most textbooks which assume you've already taken the class.

**On advice to his younger self**, the answer rhymes with the regret: *explore more.* He wishes he'd taken a year or two between undergrad and grad school, or that CS50 hadn't opened up for him quite as conveniently as it did right when he was finishing his PhD — because if it hadn't, he'd probably have gone off to industry for a while first. He acknowledges the counterfactual cuts the other way too: if he'd wandered off campus then, he probably wouldn't have gotten the CS50 job at all. So perhaps it worked out. But the GitHub sabbatical gave him a taste of the other life, and he'd be interested in more of it.
